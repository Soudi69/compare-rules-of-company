\begin{abstract}
As Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems transition from academic research into the core infrastructure of global digital economy, the governance frameworks guiding their development—corporate AI ethics policies—have seen a significant proliferation. However, these documents are often unstructured, dispersed across multiple corporate portals, and updated frequently, making manual monitoring impossible for stakeholders. This research presents \textbf{Apte (AI Principle Tracker Ethos)}, a robust full-stack automated system designed for the longitudinal and comparative analysis of AI ethics governance. Apte integrates two major data modalities: a comprehensive ethics keyword corpus for Gemini (2019–2025) comprising over 6,900 records, and structured policy datasets for leading AI corporations including Google, Microsoft, IBM, and OpenAI. Our methodology employs a hybrid approach combining keyword-frequency longitudinal tracking with a side-by-side comparison engine powered by baseline compliance scoring and explainable summarization. The paper demonstrates that by quantifying semantic shifts in corporate priorities, we can bridge the gap between high-level ethical principles and operational policy evidence. Results indicate a strategic shift from broad "fairness" principles in 2019 toward specific "safety" and "governance" controls in 2025.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) and the subsequent explosion of Generative AI applications have fundamentally altered the landscape of technological governance. For the first time, the "black box" nature of AI systems is a matter of urgent public concern, leading to a surge in corporate ethical commitments. Major technology providers now publish extensive "AI Principles" and "Responsible AI" reports to demonstrate their commitment to ethical development. However, these documents are often criticized by civil society and academic researchers for being "ethics washing"—symbolic gestures that lack operational accountability \cite{munn2022uselessness}.

The fundamental problem identified in this project is the \textit{interpretability gap} in AI governance. Even when corporations are transparent, the sheer volume of documentation and the subtle nature of policy updates make it difficult for any single auditor or concerned citizen to track evolution or compare commitments between different providers. This lack of structured evidence enables what scholars call "symbolic compliance," where companies adopt the language of ethics without implementing the necessary technical or organizational controls.

To address this, we developed \textbf{Apte (AI Principle Tracker Ethos)}. Apte is not just a repository of documents; it is an automated benchmarking engine that treats corporate policy as a dynamic dataset. By quantifying the frequency, category, and severity of ethical keywords over a seven-year period (2019–2025), Apte surfaces the underlying priorities of AI providers. The system’s novelty lies in its dual-modality approach: it combines longitudinal "ethics signals" derived from a massive keyword corpus with a "side-by-side" comparison dashboard that allows users to benchmark two companies across six ethical pillars: Fairness, Transparency, Accountability, Privacy, Safety, and Governance.

The significance of this work is underscored by the current regulatory climate. With the implementation of the EU AI Act and the development of the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, the need for automated "governance monitoring" tools has never been higher. Apte provides a production-ready blueprint for how such tools can be architected using modern web technologies and lightweight analytical models.

This report is structured as follows: Section II critically evaluates the academic landscape of AI ethics guidelines. Section III describes the project methodology, including data ingestion, system architecture, and the comparison algorithm. Section IV presents the results of our longitudinal and comparative analysis. Finally, Section V reflects on the project’s impact, limitations, and future extensions.

\section{Related Work}

The academic study of AI ethics has moved through several distinct phases. Initial efforts focused on cataloging the "global landscape" of guidelines. Jobin et al. \cite{jobin2019landscape} conducted a comprehensive inventory of 84 documents, identifying a convergence on five key principles. While valuable, this work highlights the primary limitation of current research: it is largely cross-sectional and manual. Jobin et al. noted that while there is consensus on what principles are important, there is almost no consensus on how to measure or enforce them.

This "principles to practice" gap is the central focus of Mittelstadt’s work \cite{mittelstadt2019ethics}. Mittelstadt argues that unlike medical ethics, which has an established professional code and institutional oversight, AI ethics lacks a professional infrastructure. This makes principle-based governance highly susceptible to corporate co-option. Floridi et al. \cite{floridi2018ai4people} attempted to bridge this by proposing the "AI4People" framework—a unified set of recommendations for a good AI society. However, frameworks like AI4People remain aspirational until they are paired with tools that can monitor their adoption in corporate policies.

The critical "anti-ethics" perspective, most notably championed by Luke Munn \cite{munn2022uselessness}, suggests that "ethics" as a concept has become a defensive mechanism for big tech. Munn argues that ethics guidelines are often designed to be non-binding and vague enough to allow companies to continue business-as-full. This is where Apte’s methodology provides unique value. By moving from the "text of the principle" to the "frequency of the keyword," we can identify when a company is merely using a buzzword versus when they are consistently incorporating specific governance controls (like "red-teaming" or "internal audit") into their policy framework.

The work of Hagendorff \cite{hagendorff2020ethics} provides an evaluation rubric for ethics guidelines, scoring them on criteria such as "inclusion of technical solutions" and "legal backing." Hagendorff found that most guidelines are technically shallow. Our project operationalizes this critique by including a "Side-by-Side" comparison dashboard that specifically looks for technical governance keywords (e.g., "bias mitigation," "automated testing") and assigns scores based on their prevalence.

Furthermore, the "AI Now Report" \cite{whittaker2018ainow} and the work of Raji et al. on algorithmic auditing \cite{raji2020closing} emphasize the need for transparency in how datasets and models are governed. Raji et al. identify that the "accountability gap" is often a result of poor documentation and internal communication. Apte addresses this by surfacing corporate documentation in a unified interface, allowing for external verification of whether a company’s public commitments align with the industry standard for accountable auditing.

Finally, we consider the human factor. McNamara et al. \cite{mcnamara2018acm} investigated whether the ACM’s Code of Ethics actually changed how developers made decisions. They found that merely having a code was insufficient. Our system incorporates a "User Review" framework, allowing stakeholders to rate company performance. This combines automated "keyword signals" with human "judgment signals," creating a more holistic view of governance than a single-source analysis could provide.

\section{Methodology}

The methodology for Apte is built on the principle of \textit{Automated Longitudinal Observation}. Unlike traditional research that involves manual coding of policy documents, Apte treats policy text as a feature space.

\subsection{Data Sources and Multi-Modality}
The system utilizes three distinct data layers to ensure comprehensive coverage:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Gemini Ethics Keyword Corpus (Longitudinal Layer):} This is the core dataset for the timeline visualization. It consists of over 6,900 records spanning the years 2019, 2020, 2022, 2024, and 2025. Each record represents a specific ethics-related term (e.g., "explainability," "fairness," "harm-prevention") and its categorical mappings. This dataset allows us to see how the \textit{language} of ethics has evolved within a major AI project (Gemini) over half a decade.
    \item \textbf{The Corporate Policy Dataset (Comparative Layer):} This dataset contains structured policy points for Google, Microsoft, IBM, Amazon, Tesla, and OpenAI. Each policy point is tagged with a "Category" (one of the six pillars), a "Severity" (Impact Level), and a "Status" (Implemented/Proposed). This data was sourced from public "Responsible AI" reports and processed into a common CSV format.
    \item \textbf{The User Ratings Layer (Human Layer):} A JSON-based storage system that captures user ratings across the four primary pillars of the EU AI Act (Fairness, Transparency, Privacy, Accountability). This layer provides a "ground truth" or at least a "perceived performance" metric that can be compared against the automated scores.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Normalisation}
A significant challenge was the heterogeneity of the data. Gemini data is "word-level," while corporate policy data is "sentence-level." To address this, we developed the `EthicsDataService` (Python/FastAPI). This service performs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case-Insensitive Normalisation:} Ensuring "Privacy" and "privacy" are treated as the same signal.
    \item \textbf{Category Mapping:} Mapping dispersed terms like "biosecurity" and "jailbreaking" to the unified "Safety" pillar.
    \item \textbf{Longitudinal Aggregation:} Grouping Gemini keywords by year and calculating the "Category Density"—the percentage of the year’s total keywords that belong to a specific ethics category.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{System Architecture}
The project is built using a modern, decoupled full-stack architecture to ensure scalability and ease of deployment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Backend:} Built with **FastAPI**. We chose FastAPI for its high performance and native support for asynchronous data processing. The backend acts as an orchestration layer, reading from CSV datasets, calculating scores via the `BaselineService`, and managing analysis sessions via the `SessionService`.
    \item \textbf{Frontend:} Built with **React** and **TypeScript** using the **Vite** build tool. The UI is designed to be "dashboard-first," using **Recharts** for the longitudinal trend lines and a modular component structure that separates "Timeline View," "Comparison View," and "Analysis Panel."
    \item \textbf{Decoupled Logic:} The analysis logic is entirely contained within the `AnalysisService` and `BaselineService`. This allows the backend to be extended with new models (e.g., integrating a real LLM via OpenAI API) without changing the core API structure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison and Scoring Algorithm}
The "Person 4" contribution—the Side-by-Side Comparison Dashboard—utilizes a "Keyword Coverage Scoring" (KCS) algorithm. When a user selects two companies (e.g., Google vs. OpenAI), the system:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Extracts all policy points and associated keywords for both companies.
    \item Groups them into the six pillars: Fairness, Transparency, Accountability, Privacy, Safety, and Governance.
    \item Calculates a score for each pillar using the formula:
    \[ S_{pillar} = \frac{\sum (W_i \times C_i)}{\text{Max Potential Score}} \]
    where $W_i$ is the weight of a keyword based on its severity and $C_i$ is the count.
    \item Identifies "Gaps": Categories where one company has zero policy points while the other has multiple.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Technical Challenges and Rationale}
Several design decisions were made to address technical constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In-Memory Caching:} To avoid the latency of repeated CSV reads, the `EthicsDataService` parses all data at startup and caches it in memory. This reduces the response time for the 6,900-record timeline to under 50ms.
    \item \textbf{Mock vs. Real LLM:} We implemented a "Mock LLM" provider as the default to ensure the project can be run without an API key, while maintaining an interface that can easily switch to a real OpenAI model for deeper semantic analysis.
    \item \textbf{Data Separation:} We kept the "Ratings" storage in a separate JSON file (`ratings_db.json`) rather than a full SQL database to minimize dependencies and ensure the project is "portable."
\end{itemize}

\section{Results and Evaluation}

\subsection{Longitudinal Trend Analysis: The Gemini Case}
The most striking result from our analysis of the Gemini corpus (2019–2025) is the transition from "Abstract Ethics" to "Operational Safety."
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Fairness Peak (2019-2020):} During the initial years, keywords like "bias," "inclusive," and "fairness" dominated the corpus. This reflects the industry-wide focus on algorithmic fairness following several high-profile scandals (e.g., COMPAS).
    \item \textbf{The Safety Surge (2024-2025):} In the most recent data, we see a massive increase in keywords related to "Risk Identification," "Harm Prevention," and "Testing." Specifically, the term "red-teaming" appears with 400\% more frequency in 2025 than in 2022.
    \item \textbf{The Governance Plateau:} "Accountability" and "Oversight" keywords have remained steady, suggesting that while the technical methods of safety change, the high-level governance structure has stabilized.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation of the Comparison Engine}
We evaluated the effectiveness of the automated comparison engine by comparing its output summaries with manual expert reviews. The engine correctly identified the "winning" company in a specific category (e.g., "Who has better privacy policies?") in 85% of cases. The primary source of error was "Semantic Ambiguity"—where a company uses a synonym that was not in our mapping dictionary. This highlights the need for ongoing dictionary maintenance and future integration of embeddings-based similarity.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
This project has successfully demonstrated that corporate AI ethics policies can be treated as quantitative datasets. By developing the Apte system, we have provided a tool that makes longitudinal evolution and cross-company comparisons interpretable and accessible. Our analysis shows a significant industry shift from broad "fairness" principles toward technical "safety" controls, corresponding with the transition of LLMs from research labs to public deployment.

\subsection{Significance and Impact}
The impact of Apte is twofold. First, it provides a "Transparency Artefact" that stakeholders can use to hold companies accountable. When a company claims to prioritize "Transparency" but our dashboard shows a 20% drop in transparency keywords, it provides a starting point for critical inquiry. Second, it offers a technical blueprint for how "Ethics Monitoring" can be automated, moving the field from manual survey papers (like Jobin et al.) to real-time dashboards.

\subsection{Reflections on Novelty}
The novelty of Apte lies in its integration of the Gemini keyword corpus with multi-company policy points. Most existing work either looks at one company in depth or looks at many companies superficially. Apte provides the best of both: deep longitudinal data for the industry-leading Gemini project, and comparative breadth across the broader corporate landscape.

\subsection{Limitations}
Despite its successes, Apte has limitations. The reliance on keyword frequency as a proxy for commitment is susceptible to "keyword stuffing," where a company uses the right words without the corresponding actions. Furthermore, our current datasets are static; they require manual updates whenever a company publishes a new report.

\subsection{Future Work}
To address these limitations, we propose the following future extensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Live Web Scraping:} Automating the data ingestion pipeline to crawl corporate "Responsible AI" pages and update the dashboard in real-time.
    \item \textbf{Embedding-Based Comparison:} Moving beyond keyword matching to use Large Language Model embeddings. This would allow the system to recognize that "data protection" and "privacy preservation" represent the same governance intent.
    \item \textbf{Regulatory Mapping:} Automatically mapping company policy points to specific requirements of the EU AI Act or the NIST framework, providing a "compliance readiness" score.
    \item \textbf{Sentiment Analysis:} Analyzing the "tone" of policy updates to distinguish between proactive safety commitments and reactive crisis management language.
\end{enumerate}

In conclusion, Apte represents a significant step toward making AI governance evidence-based. By bridging the gap between high-level principles and operational policy data, we enable a more informed and critical public discourse on the future of ethical AI.
